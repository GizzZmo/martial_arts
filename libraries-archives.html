2. Premier Martial Arts Libraries, Archives, and Collections

Defining "Martial Arts Library" in the Modern Context

The traditional idea of a "library" as a physical room of printed books has expanded dramatically in the digital age. For today's martial arts researcher or practitioner, a martial arts library includes traditional institutional collections, specialized digital archives of historical manuscripts, curated commercial platforms, and the official holdings of major martial arts organizations. Access methods vary widely: some resources offer free open-access scans and transcriptions, others require institutional affiliation or purchase. Understanding this ecosystem is essential for efficient research and collection building.

Institutional Libraries with Notable Martial Arts Holdings

Major university and organizational libraries remain foundational for scholarly work, providing monographs, rare books, interlibrary loan, and digital subscriptions.

SVA Library (School of Visual Arts): Primarily an art and design library, it offers useful material on the visual culture of martial arts—film, comics, illustration, and historical imagery. Its Prism Search covers both physical and many digital holdings.

University of Michigan Library: A large academic system with strong holdings in sports, recreation, and martial arts. Catalog searches under "SPORTS & RECREATION / Martial Arts & Self-Defense" surface both physical and electronic titles (e.g., Mark I. Cramer's The History of Karate and the Masters Who Made It via Ebook Central).

Kodokan Judo Museum & Library (Tokyo, Japan): The international headquarters of Judo maintains roughly 7,000 works, of which about 2,700 relate directly to Judo. It is a closed-stack collection open to students, researchers, and press. The Kodokan has made PDF catalogs of its domestic, foreign, and audiovisual archives freely available online—an important step for global researchers even when full-text download is limited.

Specialized Online Databases and Digital Archives

Specialized digital platforms have transformed access to historical martial arts literature, especially primary sources and translations that were once difficult or expensive to obtain.

Wiktenauer (The World's Largest Library of HEMA Books and Manuscripts): An indispensable open-access digital humanities project for Historical European Martial Arts. It provides scans, transcriptions, and many translations of fencing manuals (Fechtbücher) and wrestling treatises from the late medieval period through the Renaissance and beyond. Core material includes the Liechtenauer tradition, Fiore dei Liberi's Flos Duellatorum, Royal Armouries Ms. I.33 (c. 1300, the oldest known European fight book), the Talhoffer manuscripts, Paulus Kal, and George Silver's Paradoxes of Defence. Wiktenauer remains the central free repository for HEMA primary sources.

ChineseLongSword.com (Ancient Martial Arts Training Manuals): Focused on translated and interpreted Chinese weapon manuals, especially from the Ming dynasty. Key works include Cheng Zongyou's 《單刀法選》 (Dan Dao Fa Xuan – Chinese Long Saber, 1621), 《長鎗法選》 (Long Spear), and 《少林棍法闡宗》 (Shaolin Staff, 1616), plus extracts from Mao Yuanyi's 《武備志》 (Wu Bei Zhi). Materials are offered as eBooks, print books, and DVDs; selected original scans are available for free download after registration. An excellent entry point for primary Chinese historical sources in English.

Shaolin Kung Fu Online Library: Specializes in English translations of Shaolin and related Chinese martial arts texts, particularly those published in China between the 1910s and 1950s. Notable titles include Jin Jing Zhong's Authentic Shaolin Heritage: Training Methods of 72 Arts of Shaolin (1934) and Dian Xue Shu (Dim Mak) (1934), Liu Jin Sheng's Shaolin Chin Na Fa (1936), Lam Sai Wing's Southern Shaolin Hung Gar series (1957), and Xu Yi Qian's Chuan Na Quan (1936). It functions as both digital publisher and archive of translated knowledge that would otherwise remain inaccessible to most non-Chinese readers.

Project Gutenberg: Free public-domain texts, useful for older English-language works on fencing, self-defense, and Western martial practices from the late 19th and early 20th centuries (e.g., Allanson-Winn & Phillipps-Wolley's Broad-Sword and Single-Stick).

Internet Archive: A major free digital library containing scans of numerous martial arts books, training manuals, encyclopedias, and historical treatises that are otherwise out of print or hard to find. Particularly valuable for older English and translated works.

HROARR: A long-standing HEMA resource site offering downloads of historical manuals (often organized by century), research articles, equipment guidance, and instructor materials. Complements Wiktenauer with practical downloadable files.

Electronic Journals of Martial Arts and Sciences (EJMAS): An early digital portal that hosted peer-reviewed and practitioner scholarship, including the Journal of Western Martial Art (JWMA). While less actively updated, its archives remain useful for older articles on Western martial traditions.

Kokusai Bujin Renmei: An online store and curated catalog of martial arts books covering Eastern and Western styles (Karate, Judo, Kung Fu, boxing, fencing, etc.). Primarily a commercial acquisition platform rather than a free archive, but useful for discovering and purchasing specialized titles.

Selected Direct Links to Primary Sources & Free Downloads

Many primary sources are best accessed via the archive pages above (individual treatise pages on Wiktenauer contain high-resolution scans and transcriptions). Below are useful starting points and known free/public-domain downloads. Always respect the license terms of each site (some Wiktenauer scans are non-commercial only).

  • Wiktenauer PDF files — many full PDFs are hosted (use the site’s MIME/PDF search or individual treatise pages)

Chinese / Asian

  • ChineseLongSword free library — register for free original scans of Ming manuals (Long Saber, Spear, Shaolin Staff, etc.): chineselongsword.com (look for “Gain Instant Access” / free download sections)
  • Shaolin Kung Fu Online Library — commercial translations; check site for any free samples: shaolinkungfulibrary.com
  • Miyamoto Musashi – Go Rin No Sho / Book of Five Rings — various free/borrowable editions on Internet Archive, e.g. archive.org Book of Five Rings and another IA mirror

Public Domain / General

  • Project Gutenberg — search “fencing”, “broad-sword”, “self-defence”: gutenberg.org
  • Internet Archive martial arts search — broad free scans and borrowable books: archive.org search
  • Internet Archive – Fechtbuch / fencing manuals — useful keyword search: archive.org “fechtbuch”

Tip: For the highest-quality primary-source experience, start on the individual treatise page on Wiktenauer or ChineseLongSword rather than hunting isolated PDFs. Licenses and availability change; verify current terms on each site. Prefer Wiktenauer for HEMA primary sources because it provides scans + transcriptions + translations in one place.

General Digital Platforms with Martial Arts Content

Large-scale platforms remain essential discovery tools even when they are not martial-arts-specific.

Google Books: Massive digitized corpus with full-view public-domain works and previews of copyrighted titles. Often the fastest way to locate a book, sample chapters, and identify editions.

LibraryThing: Social cataloging site. Classification searches (e.g., Melvil Decimal 796.8153 for Karate) and user collections help surface popular and influential titles within specific arts.
